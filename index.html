Which pages to review first: a refresh-priority model on FlyRank search data

Abstract

Content teams cannot review every page, so they need a defensible way to decide which pages to look at first. We ask whether pre-cutoff search signals can flag pages likely to lose impressions faster than their client's typical page over the next four weeks. We built a leak-free, multi-cutoff panel from weekly Search Console aggregates and trained a gradient-boosted classifier, validated forward in time and on clients it had never seen. On that strictest test the model reached AUC 0.632 against 0.609 for a volatility baseline (client-bootstrap 95% CI for the gain: +0.005 to +0.056), with the gain concentrated in the top risk decile (1.61× average decline rate vs 1.26×). We turn the scores into a ranked review list with rule-based reason codes, and report that only two of the codes showed a clear association with decline.

Introduction

The decision this work supports is which pages a content team should review for a refresh this month. Reviewing is costly, so the goal is triage: put the pages most likely to underperform their peers at the top of the queue.

Across the panel, total weekly impressions fell from roughly 60 million in May 2026 to roughly 46–50 million in the last three weeks. A label like "impressions fell 20%" would mostly measure that period-wide drop, so we define decline relative to the same client's typical page at the same time. We describe what we observed and make no claim about its cause.

Data

  • Release: the FlyRank ML Internship warehouse (pseudonymized), about 79 million daily rows for 70 clients, 2025-01-27 to 2026-06-30.
  • Tables: the daily content performance fact table (Search Console impressions, clicks, average position), the content dimension (type, intent, word count, search volume and similar attributes), and the 90-day query table (used only at scoring time).
  • Aggregation: daily rows were summed to weekly bins (Mondays), giving 5.28 million page-week rows. The incomplete final week was dropped.
  • Panel: 16 cutoff dates, 28 days apart (2025-04-07 to 2026-06-01). A page-cutoff row is kept if the page had at least 250 impressions in the prior 4 weeks and its client had any data in the label window. This left 528,866 rows, or 528,012 after requiring at least 30 eligible pages per client-cutoff (39 clients).
  • Excluded: the 90-day query table for training (its window overlaps late label periods), and any content update or optimization dates falling after a cutoff.

Methodology

Label. For each page and cutoff, the page is positive if its impressions in the 4 weeks after the cutoff fell below 80% of the client's median page ratio (next 4 weeks vs previous 4 weeks) at that cutoff. This relative label kept the positive rate between 26% and 36% at every cutoff, where an absolute 20%-decline label ranged from 13% to 64%.

Features (all computed from before the cutoff): recent and prior 4-week impressions and clicks, momentum, spike versus own baseline, click-through rate, average position and its change, weekly volatility, weeks active, momentum relative to the client, and content attributes such as word count, age and search volume.

Baseline. Weekly-impression volatility scaled by volume, which was the best of three simple rules (momentum and position were near chance).

Validation. Walk-forward: for each of the last four cutoffs, train on every cutoff whose label window had already ended. The strict version also holds out the test clients, so the model is scored on future periods and unseen clients at once. We ran grouped-by-client cross-validation as a secondary check and computed a client-level bootstrap for the AUC difference.

Leakage checks. Label-window columns were excluded from features. A first attempt using an unequal 30-day versus 60-day comparison produced an artificially high decline rate, and we replaced it with equal windows. The query table is used only for scoring.

Limitations and honest framing

  • Observed: pages carrying higher scores had a higher rate of relative decline in later weeks, in held-out data.
  • Directional: the improvement over a simple rule is modest, its interval is close to zero, and it is concentrated in the top decile. It is unclear how far it carries into the middle of the ranking.
  • Decision-support only: the list says where to look first. It does not show why a page declined, that a refresh would help, or anything about how any search engine ranks pages.
  • The client mix changed over time (3 clients at early cutoffs, 34 at the latest), and the period covers one overall drop in impressions, so results may not transfer to other periods.
  • Only LOW_CTR and DECLINING showed a clear association; the other codes are descriptive tags. "Impressions at stake" (risk × recent impressions) is a prioritization heuristic, not an expected loss.
